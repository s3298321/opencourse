Chapter 1

Reading pages

A PDF is read a page at a time. Each page is drawn at the width of the reading column, so narrowing the window should make the page smaller rather than make the page scroll sideways.

Marking a page as read records how far you have got. Reload the reader and the progress should still be there; the percentage counts pages, not words, so this short chapter is worth as much as the long one later on.

Chapter 5

A longer chapter

This chapter runs over more than one page, so a section in the outline covers a range of pages rather than one, and reading it through means marking more than a single page.

Spaced repetition rests on a simple observation: something recalled just before it would have been forgotten is remembered for longer than something recalled while it is still fresh. Each successful recall can therefore wait a little longer than the last, and a learner who reviews at those widening intervals spends their time on what is about to slip rather than on what they already know.

Retrieval practice makes a related point. Reading a passage again feels productive, because the text becomes familiar, but familiarity is not recall. Closing the book and trying to state what it said is harder, and the difficulty is the part that does the work. A quiz at the end of a lesson is retrieval practice in its plainest form.

Interleaving mixes kinds of problems within one session instead of practising one kind at a time. It is slower while it happens and produces better results later, because the learner has to decide which method a problem needs before applying it, and that decision is exactly what a block of identical problems never asks for.

Elaboration asks why and how: why does this step follow from the last, how does this idea connect to one already known. Explanations that a learner builds for themselves tie new material to old, and material tied to more things is found again by more routes.

Concrete examples give an abstract idea something to hold. Two or three examples that differ on the surface but share the idea underneath work better than one, because they show which features matter and which are incidental.

Dual coding pairs words with pictures — a diagram beside a description, a timeline beside a narrative. The two are remembered by different means, and either one can bring back the other.

None of these techniques is new, and none needs software. What software can do is remember the schedule, keep the questions, and notice which pages were read and which were skipped, so that the learner's attention goes on the material rather than on the bookkeeping.
